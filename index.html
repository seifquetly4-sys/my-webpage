Into the Dark

Most of the ocean has never seen the sun. Past a few hundred metres, the only light left is the light that living things make themselves. Scroll down to follow that light, zone by zone, into the deepest water on Earth.

Scroll
I

The Sunlight Zone

Epipelagic · 0–200 metres

Here there's still enough light to photosynthesize, so this thin surface layer holds nearly all the ocean's plant life and most of the fish we know by name. Bioluminescence is rare this shallow — daylight simply drowns it out. But wait until dusk, and a few residents will already begin to glow, rehearsing for the dark below.

II

The Twilight Zone

Mesopelagic · 200–1,000 metres

Sunlight fades to a blue smear and then to nothing. This is where bioluminescence stops being occasional and becomes the rule: roughly nine out of ten animals here make their own light. Most of it is blue-green, the one wavelength that still travels this far through water — so nearly everything that glows, glows the same color.

III

The Midnight Zone

Bathypelagic · 1,000–4,000 metres

No trace of sunlight reaches this far, ever. Food is scarce, pressure is enormous, and animals space themselves out to avoid competing for what little there is. Down here, light isn't decoration — it's a lure, a decoy, or a warning, and every glow means something specific to whoever sees it.

IV

The Abyss

Abyssopelagic · 4,000–6,000 metres

Near-freezing water, crushing pressure, and total darkness cover most of the planet's seafloor here — this single zone is the largest habitat on Earth by area, larger than every forest, desert and grassland combined. Life is sparse and slow, but it persists: sea cucumbers, brittle stars, and the occasional drifting glow of something that fell from far above.

V

The Hadal Zone

Trenches · 6,000–11,000 metres

Named for Hades, this is the ocean's deepest reach — narrow trenches carved into the seafloor, where the pressure would crush an unprotected human instantly. Fewer than thirty people have ever gone this deep; more people have walked on the Moon. And yet even here, snailfish and amphipods drift through water that has never once seen the sun.
